# wind-power

Renewable energy infrastructure creates local trade-offs between costs and economic opportunities, sparking debate over whether the expansion of renewables trigger electoral backlash, positive feedback, or political polarisation. Moving beyond static accounts of public acceptance, this thesis investigates whether electoral responses follow an "implementation dip", a dynamic trajectory where support drops during construction before recovering over time. Empirically testing this dynamic trajectory presents a distinct econometric challenge. Because infrastructure expansion follows a complex treatment regime that is simultaneously staggered, non-absorbing, and non-binary, creating a context outside the scope of canonical Difference-in-Differences (DiD) estimators.

To address this challenge, the thesis compares two modern DiD estimators designed to handle complex treatment regimes, and applies them to 35 years of German municipal wind turbine construction and election data. Methodologically, the analysis reveals that estimator choice, aggregation, and specification decisions shape empirical conclusions, demonstrating that despite using flexible estimators, translating complex theories into estimable treatment effects remains difficult. Substantively, evidence for an implementation dip remains inconclusive, nor is there evidence of systematic incumbency punishment. Instead, the clearest finding is a small, statistically significant decline in Green Party vote shares, indicating a partisan dynamic rather than simple localised backlash. These findings warrant cautious interpretation given their sensitivity to parallel trends violations. Ultimately, the thesis highlights a tension between the conceptual simplicity of social science theories and the complexity of testing them empirically, showing that progress in understanding green transition politics requires precise theoretical operationalisation alongside methods capable of identifying dynamic effects in complex treatment environments.
